# Fact verification — The Captain's Hat

## Verified story

- Robert Smalls was enslaved and forced to work aboard the Confederate transport steamer Planter in Charleston.
- In the early morning of May 13, 1862, after the white officers went ashore, Smalls and fellow freedom seekers took the vessel, collected family members and escaped through Charleston Harbor.
- Smalls wore the captain's coat and broad straw hat and imitated his familiar pose. The 1883 congressional report explicitly describes folded arms at the pilothouse window. A fellow freedom seeker, Abram Allston, is identified by NPS as steering during the disguised passage.
- The crew gave the required steam-whistle signals to pass the Confederate defenses. Women and children remained concealed below deck during the passage.
- After clearing the forts, they raised a white sheet/flag and approached the Union blockade. They surrendered the ship and reached freedom.
- Smalls later served parts of five terms in the U.S. House: 1875–1879, 1882–1883 and 1884–1887. His later legislative service is distinct from the 1862 escape.

## Reconstruction limits

The cartoon is an illustrated reconstruction. Faces, precise gestures, dialogue-free reactions, camera positions, deck arrangement and the congressional room are staged; they are not documentary images. Smalls and Hannah are named historical people; other foreground characters illustrate fellow freedom seekers, a child and guards rather than a complete passenger census. Sources disagree on exact counts, so the narration omits passenger and fort counts. No first-person quotation is attributed to Smalls. The closing reference to the hat is narrator commentary; no escape hat is shown on display in Congress. No cannon fire is depicted during the successful escape.

The narrator's closing lines and the request to imagine keeping an ordinary face are original commentary, not recorded historical speech.
